Product Strategy · Field Notes

How to Fix a
Product Strategy
(and Why Most Fail)

Most product strategies don't fail because they're wrong. They fail because they're not usable. This is how I diagnose and fix them.

Author
Taro Naruse
Context
Across industries and company stages — from Series B to public scale
Core Argument
If your strategy isn't shaping daily decisions, it's not a strategy

If your strategy isn't actively shaping the decisions individuals make every day — it's not a strategy.

The Diagnosis

How to spot a broken strategy

Four signals that your strategy is not functioning as a decision-making tool — the core issue in nearly every case.

01
Ask 10 people. Get 10 answers.

If the team can't align on what the strategy is, it isn't functioning as one. Strategy is only real if it's shared.

02
Teams can't explain the why.

When individuals can't articulate why they're working on something, the strategy has failed to connect to execution.

03
Strategy is never referenced.

A strategy that isn't invoked in day-to-day decisions isn't guiding the org — it's theater. Decoration, not direction.

04
It sounds like a mission statement.

Inspirational language that doesn't force trade-offs isn't strategy. If it can justify everything, it justifies nothing.

Most Common Failure Modes

Three ways strategy breaks down

These patterns appear across companies at every stage. Understanding them is the first step to fixing them.

Failure Mode 01

Strategy ≠ Mission

Mission is inspirational, long-term, and broad. Strategy is focused, time-bound, and grounded in reality. Conflating the two produces language that inspires but doesn't guide.

If it doesn't force trade-offs, it's not strategy.
Failure Mode 02

No link to execution

Strategy and execution are not separate layers — they operate in a loop. Strategy guides execution; execution validates and refines strategy. Break the loop and strategy becomes theater.

If teams aren't invoking strategy unprompted, it's disconnected.
Failure Mode 03

It doesn't help you say no

A good strategy makes the important things obvious — and makes distractions easy to dismiss. Choosing between priorities is hard. Choosing between priorities and distractions should be easy.

What matters. What doesn't. That's the test.
The Fix

Four steps to a usable strategy

This is the framework I use to rebuild strategy when teams have traction but can't translate it into consistent execution.

1

Set a real time horizon

Lock the strategy to 12 months. Anything longer becomes speculation — you're guessing too far out. Anything shorter becomes tactics — you're not setting direction at all. Twelve months is the window where strategy is both grounded and meaningful.

The Rule

12 months. Not 3 years. Not 90 days. Strategy operates in the space between.

2

Define success — 3 outcomes max

Ask: what must be true in 12 months for us to win? Pick three measurable outcomes. No more. Constraint is the point. If everything is important, nothing is. Three outcomes forces the organization to agree on what winning actually looks like.

The Question

"What must be true in 12 months for us to win?" Three answers. Measurable. No more.

3

Identify the drivers

Ask: what actually causes these outcomes to happen? This is the hardest and most important step. It forces real conversations about customers, competition, and company capabilities. It surfaces assumptions — which are hypotheses, not truths. If you can't articulate the drivers, you don't have a strategy.

Why It's Hard

This step requires data and debate. Most orgs skip it because it's uncomfortable. That's exactly why most strategies fail.

4

Push strategy down to teams

Once drivers are clear, teams own how they move those drivers. Roadmaps become aligned by design. Trade-offs become consistent across the org. This is how you get top-to-bottom alignment — through empowerment, not micromanagement.

What This Unlocks

Teams reference strategy without being asked. Decisions are faster. Trade-offs are clear. Strategy becomes the operating system of the company.

What Good Looks Like

Strategy working as intended

When strategy is functioning correctly, these things become true without effort — they emerge from the system.

→

Unprompted invocation

Teams reference strategy in conversations you're not in. Decisions cite it without being asked. It has become the shared language of prioritization.

→

Faster, more consistent decisions

Trade-offs that used to require escalation get resolved at the team level. Alignment happens earlier because the direction is clear.

→

A continuous feedback loop

Learnings from execution feed back into strategy continuously. The strategy evolves because the system is designed to learn, not just to execute.

Final Thought

Strategy is not a document.
It's a shared understanding
of how the business wins.

And it shows up in every decision — not the ones made in offsites, but the ones made on a Tuesday afternoon when no one's watching.
